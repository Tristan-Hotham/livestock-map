There are about {{world.animals|bn}} farm animals alive on Earth at any moment, {{world.animals_pp|f1}} for every person. I mapped where they live, every 10 km, and how the herds and flocks have changed since {{meta.first|raw}}. Spoiler: it is the century of the chicken, and New Zealand is no longer the sheep country it was.

Ask someone to picture the world's farm animals and you get a handful of postcards: sheep on a New Zealand hillside, cattle on the pampas, pigs in Denmark. I wanted the whole picture, so I put it on one map. The FAO's Gridded Livestock of the World estimates how many cattle, buffaloes, sheep, goats, pigs, chickens, ducks and horses lived in every 10 km square in 2015, by spreading census counts across the land with a statistical model. FAOSTAT adds every country's count of eighteen kinds of farm animal, every year from {{meta.first|raw}} to {{meta.last|raw}}.

The headline numbers are startling on their own. In {{meta.last|raw}} there were about {{world.sp.cattle.n|bn}} cattle, {{world.sp.sheep.n|bn}} sheep, {{world.sp.goats.n|bn}} goats and {{world.sp.pigs.n|m0}} pigs. And there were {{world.sp.chickens.n|bn}} chickens: {{world.sp.chickens.pp|f1}} for every person on the planet.

A world of cattle, with islands of pigs and sheep

To compare a cow with a chicken you need a common unit. Farm statisticians use livestock units, a rough measure of size and appetite: on FAOSTAT's world averages a cow is about {{grid.coef.cattle|f2}} of a unit, a sheep {{grid.coef.sheep|f2}} and a chicken {{grid.coef.chickens|f2}}. Weighted that way, cattle are the main farm animal in {{main.counts.cattle|int}} of {{main.n|int}} countries and lead on {{grid.dom_pct.cattle|pc}} of the world's land. The exceptions are the interesting part: the great pig belt of eastern China, buffaloes along the Ganges and the Indus, sheep in Patagonia, the Karoo and Wales, goats on the edge of the Sahel.

The chicken century

Since {{meta.first|raw}} the number of people has grown {{world.pop_x|f1}} times. Chickens have grown {{world.sp.chickens.x|f1}} times, from {{world.sp.chickens.n0|bn}} to {{world.sp.chickens.n|bn}}, and ducks {{world.sp.ducks.x|f1}} times. Goats have more than tripled, mostly in Africa and Asia. Cattle grew more slowly than people, so there are fewer per head than there were: {{world.cattle_pp0|f2}} per person in {{meta.first|raw}}, {{world.cattle_pp|f2}} now. Horses and mules, the old working animals, have declined.

Look closely at the pig line and there is a notch in 2019. That is African swine fever in China, where the pig herd fell by {{pigs.chn_fall_pct|pc}} in a single year, about {{pigs.chn_fall_n|m0}} pigs. It was enough to knock {{pigs.fall_pct|pc}} off the whole world's total. China's herd has since recovered.

Where animals outnumber people

In {{mam_pp.n_more|int}} of the {{mam_pp.n_countries|int}} countries with at least a million people, farm mammals outnumber humans. Mongolia is in a league of its own, with {{mongolia.pp|f1}} sheep, goats, cattle, horses and camels for every person. Chad, New Zealand, Uruguay and Australia follow. Uruguay has {{pp.cattle.top.0.pp|f1}} cattle per person, the most anywhere, and Denmark is the only sizeable country with more pigs than people.

And then there is New Zealand, home of a thousand sheep jokes. Its flock peaked in {{nz.peak_year|raw}} at {{nz.peak|m}}, {{nz.peak_pp|f1}} sheep for every New Zealander. Today it is {{nz.last|m}}, {{nz.last_pp|f1}} per person, down {{nz.fall_pct|pc}}. Still a lot of sheep, but the joke is now forty years out of date.

Half of the world's pigs live on {{grid.conc.pigs.land50_pct|f1}}% of its land. Half of its cattle need {{grid.conc.cattle.land50_pct|f1}}%.

One country's pigs, another's buffaloes

Some animals belong mostly to one country. China has {{conc.pigs.top1_pct|pc}} of the world's pigs, {{conc.ducks.top1_pct|pc}} of its ducks and {{conc.geese.top1_pct|pc}} of its geese. India has {{conc.buffalo.top1_pct|pc}} of the buffaloes. A quarter of all camels live in Chad. Grazing animals are shared more widely: the biggest cattle herd, Brazil's, is {{conc.cattle.top1_pct|pc}} of the world's. On the ground the same split appears. Pigs and chickens crowd together in sheds near feed and cities; cattle and sheep spread out over grass and hill.

Britain and Ireland: sheep hills and pig plains

Closer to home, the United Kingdom has {{uk.fao.sheep|m}} sheep, more than any other European country except Turkey, but fewer sheep than people these days ({{uk.sheep_pp|f2}} each). The map splits the country by its hills. Wales has {{uk.wales_area_share|pc}} of the UK's land and {{uk.nations.Wales.sheep_share|pc}} of its sheep, and Powys alone has {{uk.top_sheep_n.0.share|pc}} of them, around {{uk.top_sheep_km2.0.per_km2|int}} for every square kilometre. Cattle are densest in Northern Ireland and the dairy country of Cheshire, Cornwall and Somerset. Pigs and chickens belong to the flat east: the East Riding of Yorkshire, Norfolk and Suffolk for pigs, Lincolnshire and, out west, Herefordshire for chickens. Across the Irish Sea, Ireland has {{ireland.cattle_pp|f1}} cattle for every person.

How I did this

The 10 km map is the FAO's Gridded Livestock of the World, version 4, for 2015: census counts from thousands of districts, shared among the squares inside each district by a random forest model of where animals are likely to be. It is a model, so the detail within each census district is an estimate, and the map is a snapshot of 2015. The country counts are FAOSTAT's 'stocks', the number of animals alive at one time, from {{meta.first|raw}} to {{meta.last|raw}}, set against World Bank population. To find each place's main animal I used FAOSTAT's own livestock unit factors for each country. These are animals alive at once, not animals raised in a year: a broiler chicken lives only a few weeks, so many more are raised each year. Wild animals, reindeer and fish are not included. The paper has every step and caveat.
